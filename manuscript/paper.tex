\begin{abstract}
Cancer is an evolutionary process that generates diverse cell states and complex tumor
microenvironments. Computational methods to profile this heterogeneity from bulk, single-cell and
spatial sequencing data are typically developed and benchmarked either on real patient data, which
lacks reliable ground truth, or on statistical simulations that ignore the biological process by
which the heterogeneity arises. We introduce \texttt{iscc}, an \textit{in silico} cancer center: a
Python package that grows spatially structured tumors as a selection-driven, agent-based
evolutionary process --- aberrant genomes shaped by copy-number and driver-mutation selection ---
and carries one such tumor through the medical and laboratory steps that generate data (biopsy,
tissue dissociation) into bulk and single-cell DNA and RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics,
down to aligned reads, optionally under therapy. Expression is shaped by the microenvironment, so
that spatially structured programmes such as hypoxia emerge from the tumor's own geometry. Because
every observation is tied to the tumor's ground-truth evolutionary history, spatial niche and cell
populations, \texttt{iscc} is designed as a substrate for benchmarking \emph{data-integration}
methods --- central to tumor-evolution studies yet impossible to validate on real data --- at two
scales: integrating DNA, RNA and spatial measurements of the same tumor, and integrating many
patients across a cohort while separating biological patient variation from the technical batch
effects with which it is confounded. Using this ground truth we expose failure modes that real data
cannot reveal, including environmental programmes misclassified as heritable when clones form
spatial territories --- a lineage--space confound we also confirm in real data --- and spurious
parallel evolution inferred from multi-region ``sample trees''. \texttt{iscc} uses an efficient
genotype-level engine that scales to large tumors, its assay parameters can be inferred from real
reference datasets, and its simulated data reproduce key statistical properties of real bulk,
single-cell and spatial measurements. The software is available as a command-line tool and a Python
library.
\end{abstract}


\section*{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
In computational oncology, simulation studies are frequently used to develop and test data
analysis approaches. Often, these studies rely on simplistic assumptions about the underlying
biological and data-generating mechanisms, abstracting away factors that have been shown to
matter for downstream applications. Tumors are the product of an evolutionary process in which
cells acquire genetic and non-genetic changes, compete for space and resources, and interact
with a structured microenvironment~\cite{gerlinger_intratumor_2012, black_genetic_2021,
whiting_phenotypic_2024}. The spatial architecture of a tumor in turn governs its mode of
evolution~\cite{noble_spatial_2022, waclaw_spatial_2015}, and copy-number alterations and
driver mutations shape clonal fitness~\cite{dinh_cinner_2025}. Methods that ignore this
generative process risk being validated on data that does not resemble the tumors they will be
applied to.

A large body of work simulates individual data modalities. Single-cell RNA-seq simulators such
as Splatter~\cite{zappia_splatter_2017}, scDesign2~\cite{sun_scdesign2_2021} and
scDesign3~\cite{song_scdesign3_2024} generate realistic count matrices, and spatial simulators
such as SRTsim~\cite{zhu_srtsim_2023} preserve spatial expression patterns; however, these
treat cells (or groups of cells) as exchangeable draws from statistical models, without an
underlying tissue that grows and is sampled. On the DNA side, coalescent and branching-process
simulators such as CellCoal~\cite{posada_cellcoal_2020} and CNAsim~\cite{weiner_cnasim_2023}
produce single-cell sequencing data along a tumor phylogeny, and recent agent-based DNA-seq
simulators such as SISTEM~\cite{weiner_sistem_2025} add genotype-driven selection and multi-site
metastasis, capturing clonal dynamics and tree topologies that selection produces and that
fixed-topology or neutral birth--death models miss; these, however, remain single-modality and
rely on manual parameterization rather than inference of model parameters from real data. Recent tools also begin to couple
copy-number alterations with transcriptomics for spatial data~\cite{xu_stcnasim_2024}, and
multi-modal single-cell simulators such as scMultiSim~\cite{li_scmultisim_2025} generate
expression, chromatin and spatial data with gene-regulatory and cell--cell-interaction
structure; these, however, take a cell-division or differentiation tree as input rather than
growing a selected tumor, and do not jointly emit DNA, spatial and treatment data from one
process. General agent-based modelling frameworks such as the Hybrid Automata Library
(HAL)~\cite{bravo_hal_2020} and PhysiCell~\cite{ghaffarizadeh_physicell_2018} can simulate
multicellular tumor dynamics in detail, but are not designed to emit the standardized
sequencing data modalities used by computational biologists.

What is missing is a single framework that connects these pieces (\Cref{tab:comparison}): an
evolving, spatially structured tumor whose ground-truth cell populations are carried through
realistic sampling and assay steps into multiple data modalities. A direct consequence is that there is no common
ground truth for methods that \emph{integrate} DNA, RNA and spatial measurements of the same
tumor~\cite{campbell_clonealign_2019}: generating each modality independently --- or bolting an
expression model onto a separately simulated copy-number profile --- yields cross-modal
correspondences that are either trivially aligned or biologically arbitrary, and in the worst
case circular, because the assumed copy-number-to-expression relationship is exactly what such
integration methods set out to infer. This gap has a second, cohort-scale face: analyses that
pool many patients --- recurrent-driver detection, shared-versus-private cell states, patient
stratification --- must integrate patients whose biological differences are confounded with the
technical batch effects of how their samples were sequenced, yet no simulator supplies the joint
patient-and-batch ground truth needed to test whether an integration method removes the batch
effect without erasing the biology. Furthermore, by not tying simulated data to a growth
model that can be acted upon through therapy, the interplay between data analysis and treatment
decision-making has been largely ignored.

Here we introduce \texttt{iscc}, a Python package that provides this end-to-end pipeline. Its
tumor module grows spatially structured tumors of multiple cell types under a copy-number and
driver-mutation selection model; its sample module performs biopsy and dissociation; its data
module generates bulk and single-cell DNA and RNA sequencing as well as spatial
transcriptomics; and its treatment module applies chemotherapy, targeted therapy and
immunotherapy, optionally as adaptive regimens. A distinguishing feature of \texttt{iscc} is
that it relates cell phenotype to the evolutionary process and only translates phenotype into a
gene-expression profile at the data-generation step, so that simulated transcriptomes
correspond to interacting, spatially organized cell types rather than abstract or independent
cell groups. Because all data are anchored to a known evolutionary history and cell-population
structure, \texttt{iscc} serves as a ground-truth substrate for benchmarking data-integration
methods at two scales: \emph{across modalities} within a tumor --- linking a clone's copy number
to its expression to its spatial niche --- and \emph{across patients} within a cohort --- aligning
shared biology while separating it from the confounded batch effects of sequencing. Because this
cross-modal and cross-patient structure emerges from the evolutionary and microenvironmental
process rather than being imposed, these benchmarks are non-circular; and because the whole tumor
can be acted upon through therapy, \texttt{iscc} also connects data analysis to treatment
decision-making.

\section*{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection*{Overview}
\label{sec:overview}
\texttt{iscc} provides an end-to-end simulation framework from tumor growth to molecular data
generation (\Cref{fig:overview}). The tumor growth model draws from 2D deme-based spatial
models~\cite{noble_spatial_2022} with multiple cell types and phenotypes~\cite{gatenbee_macrophage-mediated_2019},
as well as mathematical models of copy-number evolution~\cite{dinh_cinner_2025}. We consider
spatially structured, glandular-like tumors, which may include explicit normal-tissue
architecture such as the epithelial-lined ducts of ductal carcinoma \textit{in situ} (DCIS).

To generate data, \texttt{iscc} includes a sample module that simulates the biopsy and
dissociation steps applied to the tissue produced by the tumor model (\Cref{fig:overview}B).
The choices made in these steps, while often ignored during algorithm development, affect the
quality of the subsequent data. The software then builds on previous models for bulk and
single-cell DNA-sequencing data~\cite{valecha_somatic_2022, posada_cellcoal_2020}, single-cell
RNA-sequencing data~\cite{zappia_splatter_2017, sun_scdesign2_2021} and spatial
data~\cite{zhu_srtsim_2023, andersson_almaangrowmeatissue_2024} (\Cref{fig:overview}C). Each
assay can be emitted either as a count or coverage matrix or, one level deeper, as aligned
sequencing reads (FASTQ/BAM) generated through standard short-read simulators, with the same
ground-truth variants carried consistently across modalities. The parameters that control assay
realism are not hand-set: \texttt{iscc} can infer them from real reference datasets, and ships
with defaults calibrated to such fits, so that out of the box it produces data with realistic
statistical properties.

The parameters of every module can be specified via configuration files; the software is
distributed with the configuration files used to generate the results below. \texttt{iscc} can
be used as a command-line tool (\texttt{isccsim}, \texttt{isccsample}, \texttt{isccdata}) or as
a Python package in an interactive environment. To scale to large tumors, \texttt{iscc} uses a
genotype-level engine in which the population is represented as per-deme genotype counts over a
shared genotype registry rather than as individual cell objects; cells are materialized on
demand at the data-generation step.

\subsection*{\texttt{iscc} simulates tumors with realistic growth dynamics}
\label{sec:tumor}
The model simulates cells with multiple phenotypes that influence the evolutionary dynamics of
the tumor, including normal epithelial and stromal cells and, optionally, immune cells. The
spatial model can be initialized to a specific normal-tissue structure, such as ducts formed of
epithelial cells, enabling simulation of pre-invasive lesions and their progression.

Cancer-cell fitness follows a copy-number-aware selection model in the spirit of
CINner~\cite{dinh_cinner_2025}: oncogene and tumor-suppressor copies and mutations modulate the
division rate, while focal amplifications, deletions and single-nucleotide variants accumulate
along the lineage. Selection is expressed relative to the wild-type genome, so that the
configured baseline rates are recovered in the absence of driver events and only deviations
(driver mutations and copy-number changes) shift fitness.

A central prediction of spatial models of tumor evolution is that the manner of cell dispersal
governs the mode of evolution~\cite{noble_spatial_2022}. We recapitulate this in \texttt{iscc}
by sweeping the cell dispersal rate and measuring two mode indices over replicate simulations
(\Cref{fig:validation_evolution}): clonal diversity (the Shannon entropy of clone frequencies)
and spatial clonal assortment (whether neighbouring regions are dominated by the same clone).
As dispersal increases, clonal diversity falls monotonically (Shannon $5.8 \to 2.7$) while
spatial assortment rises ($0.21 \to 0.61$): low dispersal produces a finely intermixed, highly
diverse mosaic of local lineages, whereas high dispersal yields fewer clones expanding into
spatially coherent patches (clonal sweeps), consistent with~\cite{noble_spatial_2022}.

We further validated the single-nucleotide variants accumulated along the lineages against
neutral population-genetics theory, the standard by which spatial tumor-evolution and sequencing
simulators are checked~\cite{angaroni_jspace_2022, williams_identification_2016}. Under neutral
tumor growth, the bulk variant-allele-frequency (VAF) distribution is dominated by rare variants
and its cumulative form follows the $1/f$ power law $M(f) = (\mu/\beta)(1/f -
1/f_{\max})$~\cite{williams_identification_2016}. Growing neutral
tumors in \texttt{iscc} (all driver, dispersal and resistance effects set to one, so every
mutation is a passenger) and reading out the population VAF that a bulk assay would observe, we
find a rare-variant-dominated spectrum whose cumulative form is well approximated by the neutral
$1/f$ law over the subclonal range (mean $R^2 = 0.88 \pm 0.04$ over replicates;
\Cref{fig:validation_snv}). Because \texttt{iscc} is spatially explicit, the spectrum also shows
the depletion of intermediate-frequency variants reported for spatially constrained tumors, in
which local growth prevents subclones from sweeping the whole lesion~\cite{sun_between-region_2017,
chkhaidze_spatially_2019}.

We also validated the copy-number alterations against the selective pressures that shape cancer
genomes, which recurrently amplify oncogenes and delete tumor suppressors rather than drifting
neutrally~\cite{beroukhim_landscape_2010,davoli_cumulative_2013}. Under the CINner-style
selection model, a segment's population copy number should rise with its net oncogenic content
(oncogene minus tumor-suppressor positions). Pooling per-segment copy number against net
oncogenic content over replicate tumors, we find a positive relationship under selection that is
absent under neutral drift (\Cref{fig:validation_cna}). The effect size is modest because
copy-number events act per segment while drivers are distributed within segments --- amplifying a
segment helps its oncogenes but also its tumor suppressors --- so the neutral-versus-selection
contrast, rather than the absolute slope, is the signature. Further biological validation --- of
cell-type spatial composition and expression states --- is ongoing and follows the same
replicate methodology.

\subsection*{A genotype-level engine scales to large tumors}
\label{sec:scalability}
Benchmarking copy-number and phylogenetic methods requires tumors of realistic size, which is
costly for cell-by-cell agent-based simulators. \texttt{iscc} represents the population as
per-deme counts over a shared genotype registry, so that the cost of a step scales with the
number of distinct genotypes rather than the number of cells, and materializes per-cell
matrices only at the data-generation step. For large populations it additionally offers a
$\tau$-leaping update that advances all demes by a fixed time increment, firing
Poisson-distributed numbers of division, death and dispersal events per genotype instead of one
event at a time. We verified that $\tau$-leaping preserves the evolutionary outcome: across a
grown tumor it reproduces the exact-update growth trajectory, the clonal structure
(\Cref{fig:tau}, Muller plot) and the clone-size distribution (ECDF), with the only systematic
deviation a mild over-counting of rare clones that vanishes as the leap size decreases (clone-count
ratio $\tau/\text{exact}$ falling from $1.16$ at $\tau=1$ toward $1$ as $\tau\to0$). The speed-up
grows with tumor size --- negligible at a few hundred cells but $\sim$$6\times$ at $4000$ cells ---
so the approximation is enabled where it matters and the exact update is retained for small tumors.
In the well-mixed (single-deme) regime --- the setting in which single-cell DNA-sequencing phylogeny
simulators are usually benchmarked --- \texttt{iscc} grows a five-million-cell tumor in under three
minutes on a laptop, reaching the scale demonstrated by the agent-based DNA-seq simulator
SISTEM~\cite{weiner_sistem_2025} (five million cells in roughly twenty minutes). In this regime the
cost is governed by the number of distinct genotypes and generations rather than the cell count; in a
spatially structured tumor, where cells occupy many demes, per-step cost instead scales with the number
of occupied demes, so spatially resolved tumors of that size are correspondingly more expensive.

\subsection*{\texttt{iscc} generates multi-modal molecular and spatial data}
\label{sec:data}
The data module turns the cell populations captured by a biopsy or dissociation into observed
molecular data. For bulk and single-cell DNA sequencing, it generates per-gene coverage and
variant-allele counts with configurable error and allelic-dropout rates, from which
variant-allele frequencies and copy-number profiles can be derived. For single-cell RNA
sequencing, the module generates transcript counts with cell-to-cell variation in library size
and negative-binomial overdispersion. For spatial data, the module mimics the 10x Visium
platform, placing spots over the biopsied tissue slice and aggregating the transcripts of the
cells within each spot. Each modality can additionally be emitted as aligned sequencing reads
(\Cref{sec:reads}).

To verify that the simulated single-cell RNA-seq data are realistic, we compared \texttt{iscc}'s
output to a real 10x dataset (PBMC3k) using the same count-realism summary statistics that
single-cell simulators such as Splatter and scDesign are evaluated
on~\cite{zappia_splatter_2017, song_scdesign3_2024} (\Cref{fig:validation_scrna}). The simulated
data reproduce the variation in library size
across cells (coefficient of variation $0.40$ vs $0.46$ in real data), the mean--variance
overdispersion relationship, and the increase of dropout with decreasing mean expression. As
expected, the abstract gene model and smaller synthetic genome yield somewhat denser data than
a genome-wide assay; capturing the full sparsity of genome-wide measurements is a direction for
future work.

The same realism holds for the DNA and spatial modalities. For DNA, the assay reproduces the
copy-number-conditioned coverage dispersion of real sequencing (deeper, more even coverage at
higher copy number), a GC-content coverage bias, and, for single-cell panels, the
beta-binomially overdispersed allele-fraction spread at heterozygous loci together with allelic
dropout (\Cref{fig:validation_dna}). For spatial data, the per-spot library sizes are spatially
autocorrelated rather than independent: spots in the same region have correlated total counts, a
hallmark of real tissue sections, which we quantify with Moran's $I$ and reproduce when
re-simulating a fitted 10x Visium breast-cancer section (\Cref{fig:validation_visium}).

\subsection*{\texttt{iscc} reproduces spatial microenvironment structure}
\label{sec:microenv}
Beyond clonal and copy-number structure, real tumor expression is shaped by the microenvironment:
cells in a poorly perfused core become hypoxic, and paracrine signalling couples neighbouring cells.
\texttt{iscc} models this on the deme grid, so that a cell's expression depends on where it sits, not
only on its genotype. Oxygen is supplied by the vasculature --- which in a solid tumor resides in the
surrounding perfused stroma rather than inside the mass --- and consumed in proportion to local cell
density; solving the resulting reaction--diffusion balance yields a hypoxia field that is high in the
tumor core and low at the oxygenated rim, recapitulating the viable-rim/necrotic-core organization
long reproduced by agent-based tumor models such as PhysiCell~\cite{ghaffarizadeh_physicell_2018}
(\Cref{fig:microenv}). Because the vascular supply is external, a cancer-filled duct develops a
hypoxic centre even with no oxygenated margin --- the comedonecrosis of high-grade ductal carcinoma
\textit{in situ}. Hypoxia-responsive genes, and a ligand--receptor programme driven by neighbouring
cell density (in the spirit of the cell--cell-interaction models of
scMultiSim~\cite{li_scmultisim_2025}), are modulated accordingly. Crucially this microenvironmental
component is spatial but not heritable, and it perturbs the expression read-out only, leaving the
evolutionary dynamics unchanged --- so the same tumor can be generated with and without it. This
gives simulated spatial data realistic niche structure (without which spatial analyses have no ground
truth to be tested against) and, as we show below, sets up a subtle failure mode of lineage-based
analyses.

\subsection*{Biopsy and dissociation shape the sampled data}
\label{sec:sampling}
The choices made when a tumor is sampled --- where it is biopsied, how the tissue is
dissociated, and which assay is used --- shape the data before any sequencing happens, yet are
usually abstracted away. \texttt{iscc} makes these steps explicit and shows that they produce
the well-known biases of real studies (\Cref{fig:sampling}). A single biopsy captures only a
fraction of the clonal diversity of the whole tumor, and pooling more disjoint regions recovers
progressively more of it, recapitulating the multi-region under-sampling of intratumor
heterogeneity. Dissociation distorts cell-type composition relative to the true tissue ---
fragile cell types are under-recovered while robust ones are enriched (here immune cells drop
from $17\%$ to $12\%$ while cancer cells rise from $5\%$ to $8\%$) --- the kind of bias seen
between single-cell and single-nucleus protocols. Finally, a liquid-biopsy-style sampling that
preferentially captures cells with a higher dispersal phenotype enriches for the more invasive
subpopulation (here a $15\%$ shift in mean dispersal signal relative to uniform sampling).

\subsection*{Assay parameters can be inferred from real data}
\label{sec:inference}
Because realism depends on parameter values that vary across platforms and datasets,
\texttt{iscc} can infer them rather than relying on hand-tuning. Two inference problems are
exposed. First, the tumor-level evolutionary rates can be recovered by approximate Bayesian
computation: fitting a random-forest ABC~\cite{raynal_abc_2019} to summary statistics of
simulated tumors recovers known copy-number and mutation rates from held-out tumors with high
accuracy (mutation rate $r=0.97$, amplification probability $r=0.95$; $90\%$ credible-interval
coverage $92$--$100\%$; \Cref{fig:inference_recovery}). Second, each assay exposes an
\texttt{estimate} routine that fits its technical parameters --- coverage dispersion, GC bias,
allelic dropout and overdispersion for DNA; library-size variability and overdispersion for
RNA; spatial field length-scale and per-spot concentration for Visium --- by method-of-moments
and maximum likelihood. Fitting these to a dataset and re-simulating reproduces the observed
summary statistics (the DNA and Visium round-trips of \Cref{fig:validation_dna,fig:validation_visium}
are produced this way), and the estimators run against real references (PBMC3k for RNA; GIAB
HG002~\cite{zook_giab_2019}, a Tapestri panel and DLP+~\cite{laks_dlp_2019} for DNA; a 10x
Visium section for spatial). The assay defaults
shipped with \texttt{iscc} are calibrated to these fits, so the software generates realistic data
out of the box; only scale-free, unit-reconciled parameters are transferred as defaults, while
gene-set- and unit-dependent quantities are re-fit per dataset.

We further verified that this inference works against a real reference genome, following the
validation of CINner~\cite{dinh_cinner_2025}, which fits its copy-number-evolution model to the
real pan-cancer copy-number landscape. Building a reference of simulated tumors grown to several
hundred cells with $\tau$-leaping and fitting them to the per-arm copy-number landscape of breast
cancers from PCAWG~\cite{pcawg_2020}, \texttt{iscc} reproduces the real arm-level alteration frequencies
($r=0.78$) and, orthogonally, recovers a positive relationship between its inferred per-arm
selection and an external oncogenic-content score in the spirit of CINner~\cite{dinh_cinner_2025}
($\rho=0.32$; \Cref{fig:inference_realgenome}). The $\tau$-leaped reference is statistically
interchangeable with an exact-update reference for this fit, confirming that the acceleration of
\Cref{sec:scalability} does not degrade inference.

\subsection*{Read-level output reveals the difficulty of variant calling from scRNA}
\label{sec:reads}
Beyond count and coverage matrices, \texttt{iscc} can emit aligned sequencing reads. The compact
genome stores explicit per-position alleles, from which a per-cell reference sequence is built ---
duplicating and deleting segments according to copy number and substituting variant bases --- and
passed to standard short-read simulators (DWGSIM or ART~\cite{huang_art_2012} for DNA, with a
self-contained synthetic-transcriptome path and a real-template path in the spirit of
scReadSim~\cite{yan_screadsim_2023} for RNA) to produce FASTQ and, after alignment, BAM files. A
single canonical per-locus allele map and a single error-injection step are shared across all
modalities, so a variant present in a cell appears consistently --- at the same genomic position
with the same alternate base --- whether it is read out by DNA, RNA or spatial sequencing, and the
observed error rate matches the configured one without double-counting.

This shared substrate lets \texttt{iscc} expose a problem that count-level simulators cannot:
calling somatic variants from scRNA-seq is intrinsically hard (\Cref{fig:scrna_snv}). A genomic
variant is only observed in RNA reads if its gene is expressed and captured, so detection is gated
by expression --- variants in lowly expressed genes are almost never seen, and detection saturates
only for highly expressed genes. Even when detected, the observed RNA variant-allele fraction is a
biased, noisy version of the true DNA VAF, because allele-specific expression and capture distort
it. As a result the overall per-variant detection rate from scRNA remains low ($\sim$$0.2$--$0.3$)
across a range of capture fidelities. \texttt{iscc} thus provides ground-truth data on which to
benchmark scRNA variant callers and lineage-from-expression methods, with a known answer for which
mutations are detectable and why.

\subsection*{A ground-truth benchmark: a lineage--space confound in spatial lineage tracing}
\label{sec:petracer}
Because every simulated cell carries both its lineage and its spatial niche as ground truth,
\texttt{iscc} can be used to benchmark methods that analyse spatial lineage-tracing data --- the same
way scMultiSim and SISTEM validate their simulators by scoring downstream inference against a known
answer~\cite{li_scmultisim_2025, weiner_sistem_2025}. We consider the decomposition of expression into
cell-intrinsic (heritable) and cell-extrinsic (microenvironmental) programmes that spatial
lineage-tracing studies perform by measuring gene autocorrelation on the lineage tree versus in space,
as in PEtracer~\cite{koblan_petracer_2025} using Hotspot/PhyloVision~\cite{detomaso_hotspot_2021}.

Applying this decomposition to \texttt{iscc} data, where the true category of every gene is known, we
uncover a systematic confound (\Cref{fig:petracer}). When cells disperse little, clones form compact
spatial territories, so closely related cells occupy the same region --- and therefore the same niche.
A purely environmental programme (hypoxia) then acquires lineage autocorrelation and is
\emph{misclassified as heritable} by a tree-based method, even though it is not inherited at all. The
effect is graded: as the dispersal rate rises and clones intermix, lineage and space decorrelate and
the misclassification resolves (the fraction of extrinsic genes called heritable falls from
essentially all toward the neutral baseline). This is exactly the error that real data cannot
diagnose --- there is no ground truth for which genes are environmental --- and that \texttt{iscc}
exposes because it has one.

The same confound is measurable in real PEtracer tumors (\Cref{fig:petracer_real}). Its
ground-truth-free signature --- whether genes that are spatially autocorrelated also carry lineage
autocorrelation --- is strong in the territorial lineages of a mouse mammary tumor
($r = 0.4$--$0.5$) and absent in an intermixed one ($r \approx 0$), and \texttt{iscc}'s dispersal
sweep brackets the real values. A failure mode discovered with \texttt{iscc}'s ground truth is thus
both real and quantitatively reproduced, illustrating how a consistent tumor-to-data simulator can
guide the interpretation of state-of-the-art spatial lineage-tracing experiments.

\subsection*{\texttt{iscc} provides non-circular ground truth for multi-modal integration}
\label{sec:integration}
The same logic extends to methods that \emph{integrate} DNA and RNA. Two widely used tools invert the
relationship between copy number and expression: clonealign~\cite{campbell_clonealign_2019} assigns
single-cell RNA to a clone-of-origin under the model that expression is proportional to copy number,
and inferCNV / copyKAT~\cite{patel_single_2014, gao_copykat_2021} infer copy number from expression by
smoothing along the genome. Benchmarking such methods requires a consistent cross-modal ground truth,
which real data lack; but it also requires that the benchmark be \emph{non-circular}. A simulator that
layers expression onto externally supplied CNAs must hand-impose a copy-number-to-expression law --- and
if that law is the dosage model the method assumes, the benchmark merely re-tests the method on its own
assumption. In \texttt{iscc} the coupling is not imposed: it emerges from the engine's per-allele
dosage model (a neutral gene's expression scales with its copy number, modulated by selection) and, for
the spatial programmes, from the microenvironment. The method's assumption is therefore only
approximately correct on \texttt{iscc} data, and every cell additionally carries its true clone label
and true copy number.

We ran the genuine tools --- the R \texttt{clonealign} package and \texttt{infercnvpy} (the scverse
reimplementation of inferCNV), each in its own environment --- on a simulated multi-clone tumour
measured by both scDNA-seq and scRNA-seq, and scored them against \texttt{iscc}'s ground truth
(\Cref{fig:clonealign,fig:infercnv}). Given the clones' copy-number profiles from the DNA modality,
clonealign assigns scRNA cells to their true clone-of-origin with a mean one-vs-rest AUC of $0.84$
(accuracy $0.58$ across four clones against a chance level of $0.25$), from expression alone. Crucially
the assignment is driven by the emergent dosage coupling: sweeping the fraction of genes that carry a
clone-specific copy-number difference, assignment quality rises monotonically from the chance baseline
to its full value (\Cref{fig:clonealign}D), and the clones that blur together are exactly those
separated by a single altered segment. inferCNV, in turn, separates the malignant cells from the
diploid normal compartment almost perfectly (AUC $0.99$ by CNV burden) and recovers the clonal
gain/loss structure (clone-level correlation $0.86$ with the true copy number); per-cell copy-number
correlation is real but modest, reflecting the genuine difficulty of the inverse problem at single-cell
resolution. Together with the PEtracer benchmark, these demonstrate a general pattern: because
\texttt{iscc} generates every modality from one evolving tumour, it supplies the non-circular,
cross-modal ground truth that integration methods are otherwise validated without.

\subsection*{A ground-truth benchmark: multi-region bulk ``sample trees'' are not phylogenies}
\label{sec:multiregion}
The same coupling of lineage and space lets \texttt{iscc} quantify a second, long-argued error.
Alves, Prieto and Posada observed that a tree built from bulk multi-region tumor samples is not a
phylogeny: because each region is an \emph{admixture} --- a mixture of clones at different
proportions rather than an isolated lineage --- a ``sample tree'' drawn from regional mutational or
variant-allele-frequency (VAF) profiles reflects similarity, not evolutionary history, producing
spurious parallel mutations, biased divergence times and reversed ordering~\cite{alves_multiregion_2017}.
Their argument was illustrative; \texttt{iscc} supplies the missing ground-truth answer key, because
it records the true clone phylogeny (\Cref{sec:petracer}) alongside real spatial admixture (clonal
territories tuned by the dispersal rate). We grow a solid tumor, take $K$ multi-region biopsies, and
pool each region into a deep bulk-DNA sample (the admixed regional VAF profile a multi-region study
measures). From the region$\times$region genetic distances we build the neighbour-joining sample tree
such a study would draw, and score it against the truth using a metric that needs no matched leaf
set: the rate of \emph{spurious parallel mutations} --- loci the true lineage shows arose exactly
once (a single origin, verified by parsimony on the true clone tree) but that the region tree infers
as two or more independent origins (homoplasy). Restricting the count to truly single-origin loci
makes it an honest measure of the admixture artifact, excluding genuinely recurrent mutations.

The naive region sample tree invents parallel origins for $\sim$$24\%$ of single-origin mutations,
and it reverses the true ancestor--descendant ordering of $\sim$$23\%$ of nested mutation pairs
(\Cref{fig:multiregion}B). Crucially, taking more biopsies does not fix this: as $K$ grows from four
to ten regions the spurious-parallelism rate does not fall toward zero --- it rises --- because the
problem is admixture, not sampling density (\Cref{fig:multiregion}C). The error scales with the
directly measured per-region clone admixture across a dispersal sweep ($r \approx 0.7$;
\Cref{fig:multiregion}D). Applying the paper's proposed fix --- deconvolving clones per region before
building the tree, here bounded by \texttt{iscc}'s per-cell clone truth as an oracle deconvolution
--- collapses the spurious rate to $\sim$$0.4\%$ and recovers $\sim$$93\%$ of the true clone splits,
even though the resulting clone tree has \emph{more} leaves than the region tree (confirming the
artifact is admixture, not tree size). As with the lineage--space confound, this is a failure mode
that real multi-region data cannot diagnose for want of a ground truth, and that \texttt{iscc}
quantifies because it has one.

\subsection*{A ground-truth benchmark: copy-number burden manufactures spurious expression
``programs''}
\label{sec:programs}
The two preceding confounds are spatial. A third is genomic, and it reaches the other major class of
single-cell method: gene-program (or gene-expression-program) inference, in which a factor model
decomposes an expression matrix into recurrent programs --- cell cycle, hypoxia, EMT, stress --- and
their per-cell activities~\cite{gavish_hallmarks_2023}. Asking whether such a decomposition is
\emph{correct} requires knowing the true programs, which no real dataset provides. \texttt{iscc}
models expression as exactly this object (\Cref{sec:methods_expression}): a per-cell activity vector
$z$ over $K$ programs with a known gene$\times$program loading matrix, multiplied by per-gene copy
dosage, cis-SNV effects and the niche field. Both the loadings and the per-cell $z$ are recorded, so
an inferred factor can be matched to the program that generated it.

The load-bearing property is an orthogonality: programs are \emph{functional} gene sets scattered
across the genome, whereas copy-number alterations are \emph{contiguous} segments. This makes the
benchmark non-circular --- \texttt{iscc}'s forward model is not the law these tools invert --- and it
motivates a specific hypothesis. Because a CNA moves every gene on a segment together, a high
copy-number burden should induce \emph{positional} co-expression: genes co-varying because they share
a segment, not a function. A factor model has no notion of genome position and could absorb this as a
program. We test this by sweeping copy-number burden (fraction of the genome altered, FGA) and, as a
control axis, SNV burden, scoring the recovery of the true programs by \textbf{scDEF}, a hierarchical
Bayesian factor model~\cite{ferreira_scdef_2024}, against \textbf{cNMF}, the field-standard consensus
NMF method~\cite{kotliar_cnmf_2019}. Each true program is Hungarian-matched to its best inferred
factor by loading cosine; a factor resembling \emph{no} true program is counted as spurious.

The result is more specific than the hypothesis, and it splits in two. Recovery of the true programs
is largely \emph{robust} to copy-number burden: as FGA rises from $0$ to $0.55$, scDEF's mean loading
cosine falls only from $0.74$ to $0.69$ and cNMF's from $0.51$ to $0.40$ (\Cref{fig:programs}B). The
real programs survive. What copy-number burden does instead is \emph{manufacture additional factors
that are pure positional artifact}. The diagnostic that separates the two is the orthogonality
itself: for each factor we measure the largest fraction of its top-loaded genes falling in a single
genomic segment, against the null expected of a genuinely scattered gene set ($0.20$; $95$th
percentile $0.28$). At zero burden every factor of both tools sits at that null. As FGA rises, each
tool's \emph{matched} factors stay at the null ($0.20$--$0.22$ throughout, i.e.\ they remain real,
scattered programs) while its \emph{spurious} factors climb far above it --- to $0.76$ for cNMF and
$0.37$ for scDEF (\Cref{fig:programs}D). cNMF also invents more of them as burden grows (seven to
nine of twelve fitted factors; \Cref{fig:programs}E). At high FGA, three-quarters of a spurious cNMF
factor's signature genes are neighbours on one segment: it is a copy-number event wearing the label
of a biological program, and nothing in the tool's output marks it as such.

The SNV sweep is the control that makes this attributable to contiguity rather than to mutation
burden in general. Across a four-fold range of SNV burden at zero FGA, recovery is flat for both
tools ($0.71$--$0.74$ scDEF; $0.48$--$0.51$ cNMF) and the positional statistic never leaves the null
($0.19$--$0.23$; \Cref{fig:programs}C,D). Scattered mutations, however many, do not create positional
pseudo-programs; contiguous ones do. The two tools also trade off in an interesting way: scDEF
recovers the loadings better and invents fewer and cleaner spurious factors, whereas cNMF recovers
the per-cell \emph{activities} substantially better ($r \approx 0.80$ vs $0.44$ at zero burden).

Two caveats are worth stating. First, the diagnostic detects \emph{position}, not artifact. Setting
\texttt{program\_genomic\_scatter} to zero builds a deliberately CNA-mimicking program --- genuinely
contiguous, with no CNAs present --- and its correctly matched factors then score $0.37$ (scDEF) and
$0.42$ (cNMF), above the scattered null (\Cref{fig:programs}A2). A truly positional program is
indistinguishable, by this statistic, from an absorbed CNA; the statistic is a flag for review, not a
verdict. Second, the effect is measured on \texttt{iscc}'s counts, and its magnitude will depend on
how strongly dosage propagates to expression, which \texttt{iscc} deliberately buffers per gene.
Within those limits the finding is the same shape as the two confounds above --- genomic structure
misleading an inference method that has no way to see it --- and it is only visible because the
generating programs are known.

\subsection*{\texttt{iscc} implements conventional and adaptive therapy strategies}
\label{sec:treatment}
A central goal in computational oncology is to use detailed molecular profiles of tumors to
propose treatments tailored to each tumor. \texttt{iscc} includes a treatment module with
chemotherapy, targeted therapy and immunotherapy, applied as the tumor evolves on the default
genotype engine and optionally scheduled as an adaptive regimen that modulates dosing in
response to tumor burden~\cite{west_survey_2023}. Death-rate therapies (chemotherapy and
targeted therapy) impose a kill hazard on sensitive cells, attenuated by each cell's evolved
treatment resistance; immunotherapy instead strips immune resistance so that the local immune
microenvironment kills more, coupling its efficacy to immune cell density and proximity.
Targeted therapy and immunotherapy can be restricted to cells expressing specific genes,
coupling response to the simulated phenotype.

We illustrate this on a sensitive tumor grown to an established burden and then continued under
three regimens (\Cref{fig:treatment}). Without treatment the tumor keeps growing; continuous
chemotherapy regresses it to elimination; and an adaptive regimen that doses only above a burden
threshold holds the tumor in check while delivering substantially less drug (here $\sim$36\%
fewer dosed steps). Because \texttt{iscc} records the full pre- and post-treatment clone history,
it can be used to study therapy-induced resistance and to compare continuous and adaptive
strategies on a shared evolutionary ground truth.

\subsection*{Operating regimes: which parameters yield realistic tumors}
\label{sec:envelope}
Because a simulator's realism holds only within a range of its parameters, we characterize
\texttt{iscc}'s \emph{operating envelope} --- the region of parameter space that produces realistic
tumors --- and report it, in the spirit of the robustness and sensitivity analyses shipped by
scMultiSim~\cite{li_scmultisim_2025} and CINner~\cite{dinh_cinner_2025}. We swept the key evolutionary
and spatial knobs around the default configuration and, for each run, computed phenotype metrics that
identify the \emph{degenerate} regimes a user can fall into: extinction, monoclonal tumors (from too
few mutations or a runaway selective sweep), hypermutated ``mush'', spatially well-mixed tumors with
no clonal territories, and tumors with no microenvironment gradient (\Cref{fig:envelope}). The
boundaries are sharp and interpretable. A tumor goes extinct once the death rate approaches the
division rate, and a single founder cell carries an appreciable stochastic extinction risk that
seeding a small cluster removes (\Cref{fig:envelope}D). Clonal diversity collapses to a single clone
when the mutation supply is too low and grows with it (\Cref{fig:envelope}E), while an excessive
mutation rate produces a hypermutated tumor in which almost every cell is unique and the neutral
$1/f$ tail breaks. Copy-number burden rises with the amplification probability but \emph{saturates}
rather than running away, because the genome-viability caps cull non-viable karyotypes --- an
honest limit of the model rather than a tunable failure. Realistic analyses expect tumors of
$\sim$$10^3$--$10^4$ cancer cells; both structured (glandular-duct) and unstructured growth reach
this range once the arena is large enough to hold them ($\text{grid\_size}^2\times\text{carrying
\_capacity}$) and the simulation is run long enough --- in the exact engine tumor size grows by
roughly one cell per step, so the genotype-level $\tau$-leaping engine (\Cref{sec:scalability}) is
used to reach large sizes efficiently.

Two degenerate regimes are worth emphasizing because they silently break the ground-truth benchmarks
of \Cref{sec:petracer,sec:multiregion}. When the dispersal rate is large relative to the division
rate, clones no longer form compact spatial territories but smear across the whole lesion
(\Cref{fig:envelope}A,F); the lineage--space coupling on which the PEtracer confound and the
multi-region ``sample tree'' distortion depend then vanishes, so a run in this regime would produce a
null result for the wrong reason. Likewise, a tumor that is small relative to the oxygen diffusion
length develops no core--rim hypoxia contrast (\Cref{fig:envelope}C), removing the microenvironment
gradient that the intrinsic-versus-extrinsic decomposition is meant to expose. Reporting these zones
lets users --- and reviewers --- see at a glance which settings are safe.

To make this actionable rather than merely documented, \texttt{iscc} ships the same metrics as a
built-in, read-only quality-control diagnostic (\texttt{tumor.diagnose()}) that runs after growth,
flags each degenerate regime against an overridable threshold, and prints an actionable hint (for
example, ``well-mixed: lower \texttt{dispersal\_rate}''; ``monoclonal: raise \texttt{mutation\_rate}'';
``no O$_2$ gradient: grow a larger tumor''). Like the microenvironment ground truth, the diagnostic
never draws on the simulation's randomness and does not alter its output. \Cref{tab:envelope}
summarizes the default value, valid range, and out-of-range failure mode for each knob; these
defaults place the shipped configuration inside the realistic region of every axis.

Finally, the operating envelope and the parameter inference of \Cref{sec:inference} close a loop:
fitting \texttt{iscc} to data should land in the realistic region, and we verify that it does
(\Cref{fig:calibration}). Auditing the ABC search prior with the diagnostic reveals that it is almost
entirely inside the good region, and it also caught a founder-extinction bottleneck --- the inference
base configuration seeded a single founder cell, so a few percent of simulations went silently extinct
--- which we removed by seeding a small established lesion, after which the prior is fully
non-degenerate. Running ABC-rf recovery on realistic tumors, every inferred point estimate falls inside
the reported good ranges and regrows into a non-degenerate tumor. The envelope thus both documents the
usable region and actively protects the calibration and benchmarks that depend on it.

\subsection*{\texttt{iscc} provides cohort-level ground truth: shared-vs-private states and the need for personalized medicine}
\label{sec:cohort}
The benchmarks so far concern a single tumor, but many of the hardest analysis problems --- batch
integration, demultiplexing, recurrent-driver detection, patient stratification --- are defined only
across a \emph{cohort} of patients, where no real dataset can supply the ground truth: which cells came
from which patient, which cell states are shared across patients versus private to one, which mutations
recur, and which patients will respond to a therapy. \texttt{iscc} generates this cohort-level ground
truth by running a different tumor per patient (each with its own evolution seed, giving private
clones, private passenger mutations, and patient-specific copy-number and spatial structure) over one
\emph{shared, config-determined} driver landscape. The shared landscape is a prerequisite fix to the
engine: the abstract genome's gene-role layout (which loci are oncogenes, tumor suppressors, resistance
genes) and the baseline per-cell-type expression are drawn from a config-determined \emph{layout} seed,
decoupled from the per-run \emph{evolution} seed. Without this decoupling, two runs of the same
configuration place their drivers at different loci, so cross-patient recurrence is undefined; with it,
driver identities are identical across patients by construction while the stochastic evolution stays
private (\Cref{fig:cohort}A: mean pairwise driver-set Jaccard $=1.0$ with the fix versus $\approx0.04$
with per-seed layouts). This mirrors, in abstract mode, the fixed shared landscape the real-genome
mode already provides, and makes the cohort the unit of inference. We surface as ground truth the
recurrent-versus-private driver split, per-cell patient-of-origin, shared-versus-private cell-state
labels, and per-patient molecular subtype with its true therapy response --- exactly the
``simulator-provides-the-truth'' convention of scMultiSim~\cite{li_scmultisim_2025} and
SISTEM~\cite{weiner_sistem_2025}, extended from one tumor to a cohort. Patients are emitted into
sequencing batches through a flexible patient-to-batch map --- one patient per batch, or several
patients multiplexed into a shared lane up to a technical capacity (cell-hashing / genetic-multiplexing
style) --- by reusing the multi-batch scRNA machinery, so each batch carries both biological (different
patients) and technical variation.

This cohort ground truth turns four otherwise-unfalsifiable analyses into scorable benchmarks
(\Cref{fig:cohort}). \emph{Personalized medicine} is the headline, and here resistance is not imposed
but \emph{emerges}: we define two molecular subtypes over the same landscape that differ only in a
resistance \emph{effect}, and during an untreated burn-in resistance mutations arise spontaneously at
the shared resistance loci and drift as neutral standing variation (they are inert without drug).
Adjuvant therapy then selects them --- the high-effect (resistant) subtype relapses from its
standing resistant cells while the low-effect (sensitive) subtype, in which the same mutations are
inert, is eradicated (\Cref{fig:cohort}B). The differential response is therefore a genuine
evolutionary outcome with a known answer key, not a hand-imposed one. Recovering the responsive
subtype from molecular data reproduces a real precision-oncology difficulty: at \emph{baseline} the
two subtypes are molecularly indistinguishable (the standing resistance mutations are present in both;
only their functional effect differs), so a bulk baseline call is non-predictive (area under the curve
$\approx0.5$); the responder is instead recovered from the therapy-selected \emph{emergent} signature
(the relapsed tumor is clonally enriched for the selected resistance mutations, AUC $\approx0.8$) and
from the response itself (AUC $1.0$) --- a distinction \texttt{iscc}'s per-cell ground truth makes
explicit. \emph{Multi-patient
integration} (\Cref{fig:cohort}C) exposes the central over/under-correction failure mode: with one
patient per batch, a naive embedding over-separates the \emph{shared} cell states by patient (low
integration-LISI), and a batch correction restores cross-patient mixing of shared states while
preserving the private tumor biology (cell-type adjusted Rand index unchanged) --- scored against the
shared-versus-private labels, with real Harmony/scVI usable in a dedicated environment.
\emph{Demultiplexing} (\Cref{fig:cohort}D) is modeled per modality, because the two sequencing
modalities are demultiplexed differently in practice. DNA assays (WGS/WES/scDNA) cover the genome or
exome broadly, so pooled cells are demuxed \emph{genetically} from their germline SNPs
(souporcell/vireo/demuxlet) --- \texttt{iscc} carries the germline background in every cell of an
individual (tumor \emph{and} normal), so every cell, cancer or normal, is assigned to its
patient-of-origin. Droplet scRNA, by contrast, covers only sparse expressed loci and cannot genotype
germline SNPs per cell reliably, so pooled scRNA is demuxed by \emph{cell hashing} --- a per-sample
oligo barcode (Cell Hashing / MULTI-seq); \texttt{iscc} emits the hashtag readout (own tag $+$ ambient
soup $+$ doublets) and recovers singlets near-perfectly, with the true challenge being the doublet
detection that cell super-loading demands (naive accuracy falls with the doublet rate while the
doublets stay detectable, \Cref{fig:cohort}D inset). Finally, \emph{recurrent-driver detection} is
well-posed only because the landscape is shared: driver genes recur more than private passengers
(\Cref{fig:cohort}A, inset), though in abstract mode the per-gene enrichment is modest because fitness
depends on the \emph{count} of mutated drivers rather than their identity --- the real-genome arm model,
which concentrates selection on specific chromosome arms, is the sharper substrate for that benchmark.

\subsection*{A ground-truth benchmark: which planted evolutionary dependencies can cohort progression models recover?}
\label{sec:epistasis}
Cohort progression models --- MHN~\cite{schill_modelling_2020}, TreeMHN~\cite{luo_joint_2023},
conjunctive Bayesian networks~\cite{gerstung_quantifying_2009} and
REVOLVER~\cite{caravagna_detecting_2018} --- pool many tumors to recover a single \emph{network} of
promoting, inhibiting and ordering dependencies between evolutionary events. Benchmarking them needs
a known network, which a real cohort never provides. \texttt{iscc}'s selection is additive by default
(fitness reads the \emph{count} of mutated drivers), so its true network is empty and such a benchmark
would measure only a method's false-positive rate. We therefore extended selection so that a genotype's
fitness depends on \emph{which} events co-occur: log fitness gains
$\sum_i \beta_i x_i + \sum_{i<j} E_{ij} x_i x_j$ over the genotype's event vector $x$, optionally with
a conjunctive dependency DAG (\Cref{sec:methods_tumor}). The interaction is a pure function of the
event set, so it caches per genotype and is unchanged under tau-leaping; with no network configured
the engine is bit-identical to the additive model. Crucially the network is drawn from the
\emph{layout} stream, so every patient in a cohort evolves under the \emph{same} network --- the
premise that makes pooling patients to fit one model well-posed at all --- while their evolution stays
private.

Planting the answer key turns a specificity check into a real benchmark, and what it exposes is that
the \emph{observable} is what decides recoverability (\Cref{fig:epistasis}). Pairwise epistasis $E$
acts on \emph{fitness}: it changes how large the clones carrying a combination grow, not the rate at
which the events arise. In a paired design in which only $E$ differs, that effect is large and
monotone --- raising $E$ from $0$ to $1.5$ expands the clones carrying the favoured pair from 3.5\% to
42\% of the tumor (a 12-fold gain), plateauing at 59\% once the planted strength exceeds the
division-rate cap $\log(b_{\max}/b_0)$, beyond which selection has no headroom to express it
(\Cref{fig:epistasis}B). The probability that the pair ever \emph{arose} barely moves (0.03 to 0.09),
as expected: that is a property of mutation, not of selection. Yet a binary ``which events did this
patient ever acquire'' matrix --- what MHN consumes --- registers almost none of the 12-fold effect,
and the reason is recurrent mutation rather than clone size \emph{per se}: a favoured combination
arises many times independently within one tumor, so it is already present at $E=0$ and the column
saturates. What selection changes is how much of the tumor those lineages occupy, which is precisely
what a presence call discards.

Running the tools themselves is what disciplines this claim, and it also identifies precisely which
signal each one can see. The two consume different projections of the same cohort: MHN reads binary
event presence, discarding both order and frequency, whereas TreeMHN reads mutation-tree topology,
which retains the order in which events arose along each lineage but --- contrary to a natural
expectation --- still not clone sizes, since its input is the tree alone. TreeMHN's advantage over
MHN is therefore \emph{order}, and fitness epistasis generates no order: it changes how large the
carrying clones grow, leaving the sequence of events untouched. The prediction is that TreeMHN cannot
beat MHN on pairwise $E$ for structural reasons rather than want of data, and that it should win
decisively once the planted signal \emph{is} an order. Both hold (\Cref{fig:epistasis}D). On pairwise
$E$, TreeMHN sits at chance (mean rank 3.4 of six pairs, chance 3.5) while MHN reaches 1.6 --- but
MHN's empty-$E$ control itself ranks the pair 2.0, far above chance, so most of that is a
false-positive tendency an untested benchmark would have mistaken for recovery (4 of 5 draws versus 1
of 5, Fisher $p = 0.21$). On an accessibility-gated dependency, which is a genuine order signal, the
ordering reverses and sharpens: TreeMHN attains mean rank 1.8 and recovers the planted edge outright
in 3 of 5 draws, while MHN falls to 4.0 --- below chance --- and the co-occurrence floor to 5.4. The
one draw in which TreeMHN fails is the one in which a single patient carried the gated event, i.e.
where there was no signal to read. Sweeping the detection threshold on cancer-cell fraction
(\Cref{fig:epistasis}C) shows why the frequency route is hard in this regime: at zero threshold the
observable is saturated and at a high one it is empty, with only a narrow window between. These
numbers are a floor rather than an estimate for real cohorts --- our tumors are $\approx$130 cells,
small enough that a clone arising late has little time to expand, so much of the frequency signal
never develops --- but the mechanism (\Cref{fig:epistasis}B) and the tool--signal correspondence
(\Cref{fig:epistasis}D) are structural and should survive scaling.

\subsection*{Outlook: from benchmarking to experimental design}
\label{sec:outlook}
Because \texttt{iscc} connects a known evolutionary ground truth to the data an analyst actually
observes, it can be run not only forwards --- to generate benchmark data --- but also as a planning
tool. Given a pilot dataset to fit assay parameters (\Cref{sec:inference}), a scientific goal, and a
budget, the same engine can simulate candidate experiments, run the intended downstream analysis,
and score which designs recover the truth. This generalizes single-modality power-analysis tools
such as scPower~\cite{schmid_scpower_2021} to a goal-agnostic, multi-modal setting that also spans
sampling geometry and treatment monitoring. We develop this ``recommender'' mode in a companion
paper; here we establish the consistent generative engine on which it rests.


\section*{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}
In the following we outline the modules that constitute the package: tumor, treatment, sample
and data, together with the inference routines that fit them to real data.

\subsection*{The \texttt{tumor} module}
\label{sec:methods_tumor}
This module contains the building blocks of a tumor as classes: \texttt{Genome},
\texttt{Cell}, \texttt{Deme} and \texttt{Selection}. A tumor can be instantiated as a glandular
(spatially structured) model. Demes are arranged on a 2D grid; each holds cells up to a
carrying capacity, and cells divide, die and disperse to neighboring demes at rates determined
by their genotype and local environment. The genome of each cell is represented compactly, with
copy number tracked per segment and single-nucleotide variants stored as per-position bitsets
under an infinite-sites assumption. We follow the copy-number and driver-mutation selection
model developed in CINner~\cite{dinh_cinner_2025}: oncogene and tumor-suppressor wild-type and
mutated copies, normalized by ploidy, multiplicatively modulate the division rate, and analogous
terms govern dispersal and resistance phenotypes. At each mutating division a
Poisson-distributed number of single-nucleotide variants is placed genome-wide, with the
expected count scaling with the number of alleles present so that more amplified genomes acquire
proportionally more variants. To scale, the simulation is run at the level of genotypes:
identical cells of a clone in a deme are represented by a count over a shared genotype, and the
per-cell matrices required for data generation are materialized on demand. For large
populations an optional $\tau$-leaping update advances all demes by a fixed time step, drawing
Poisson numbers of division, death and dispersal events per genotype; it preserves the growth
trajectory, clonal structure and clone-size distribution of the exact update
(\Cref{fig:tau}) while the speed-up grows with tumor size.

Selection is additive in this base model. An optional \emph{epistasis} layer (\Cref{sec:epistasis},
off by default and bit-identical to the additive model when unused) makes fitness depend on which
events co-occur. An \emph{event} is a disjoint module of driver genes, acquired when at least one
variant lands in the module and monotone thereafter --- the generative assumption the progression
models are defined under. Log division fitness gains $\sum_i \beta_i x_i + \sum_{i<j} E_{ij} x_i x_j$
over the genotype's binary event vector $x$, where $\beta$ are the events' marginal effects and the
sparse symmetric $E$ carries the planted interactions (positive: synergy; negative: antagonism;
strongly negative: mutual exclusivity / synthetic lethality). Conjunctive constraints are a layered
random DAG over the events, applied in one of two modes: \emph{fitness} gating, where a child event
arises freely but contributes no $\beta$ until all its parents are present, or \emph{accessibility}
gating, where variants in a child's module are vetoed until its parents are present. Because the
term depends only on the event set, it is cached per genotype and is identical under the exact and
$\tau$-leaping updates. The network is drawn from a dedicated sub-stream of the configuration-determined
\emph{layout} seed rather than the per-run evolution seed, so that every patient of a cohort shares one
network (and so that changing the network does not perturb the gene-role layout); an explicit distinct
layout seed draws an independent network for replicate studies. The true $E$, DAG, exclusive pairs and
the realized per-lineage event order --- with events acquired in the same division recorded as tied,
since no order between them exists --- are exposed as the answer key. Two observables are exported
from the same cohort: the binary patients $\times$ events matrix MHN consumes, in which an event is
called when its cancer-cell fraction (summed across all carrying clones, as a variant-allele-frequency
call would) exceeds a detection threshold; and the per-patient mutation trees TreeMHN consumes, whose
topology encodes the order in which events arose but not clone sizes (its input accepts the tree
alone). MHN and TreeMHN are run at their published defaults in dedicated environments, and
because their $\Theta$ is a rate modifier rather than a fitness coefficient we score recovered
\emph{edges} (symmetrized over the two directions, since $E$ is symmetric) rather than values.

\subsection*{Gene-program expression}
\label{sec:methods_expression}
Expression is modelled as a program backbone with two gene-level genotype overlays. Each cell carries
an activity vector $z$ over $K$ programs, and a program's effect on a gene is given by a
gene$\times$program loading matrix, so that for allele $a$ of gene $g$
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{exp}_{g,a} = \frac{\mathrm{base}_{t,g}}{2}\cdot
  \exp\!\Big(\textstyle\sum_k z_k\,\mathrm{loading}_{k,g}\Big)\cdot
  \mathrm{dosage}(\mathrm{CN}_{g,a};s_g)\cdot \mathrm{snv}(\mathrm{class}_{g,a})\cdot \mathrm{niche}_g ,
\end{equation*}
with total expression $\mathrm{exp}_g=\mathrm{exp}_{g,p}+\mathrm{exp}_{g,m}$ and the RNA B-allele
frequency $\mathrm{exp}_{g,p}/\mathrm{exp}_g$. Keeping the two homologs separate, rather than summing
them, is what makes allele-specific expression and the RNA BAF available at all --- the signal
allele-aware integration methods depend on. Program genes are drawn scattered genome-wide by default
(\texttt{program\_genomic\_scatter}), which is the orthogonality to contiguous copy-number segments
that \Cref{sec:programs} exploits; setting it to zero builds a deliberately CNA-mimicking program.

Copy number reaches expression through a per-gene dosage sensitivity $s_g\in[0,1]$ ---
$\mathrm{dosage}=1+s_g(\mathrm{CN}-1)$, optionally saturating, and identically zero for a deleted
homolog --- so the copy-number-to-expression law is only \emph{approximately} the linear one that
dosage-based integration methods assume, and per-gene buffering is a genuine confounder rather than a
tautology. Single-nucleotide variants are assigned a functional class per site (silent, missense,
splice, or loss-of-function subject to nonsense-mediated decay), and their expression effect is
parameterized \emph{separately} from their fitness effect; a loss-of-function variant on one homolog
combined with copy loss of the other yields biallelic silencing without a special case.

The genotype couples to programs at three separable levels. By default the coupling is
phenotype-mediated: each evolved per-clone phenotype drives one program (division rate
$\rightarrow$ proliferation, dispersal $\rightarrow$ EMT, treatment resistance $\rightarrow$ drug
resistance, immune resistance $\rightarrow$ immune evasion), so a mutation raises a program through
the fitness model already in place, and clone identity reaches expression by a route that is not
dosage. A sparse tagged subset of genes may instead shift $z$ with no fitness change, and niche fields
may drive designated programs. Programs are a readout only: they never feed back into fitness, so
growth is unchanged whether the layer is on or off. A phenotype is a property of a clone and therefore
sets a program's \emph{mean} across that clone's cells, while per-cell activity supplies within-clone
heterogeneity; \texttt{tumor.diagnose()} flags the degenerate regime in which that spread vanishes and
a clone becomes its own expression state. The program dictionary, the regulator assignment and $s_g$
are properties of the genome and are drawn from the configuration-determined layout stream (in
independent sub-streams), so patients sharing a configuration share their programs exactly as they
share their driver landscape, while per-cell activity and each variant's class are per-run draws.

\subsection*{The \texttt{treatment} module}
This module includes chemotherapy, targeted therapy and immunotherapy. Each treatment is applied
during \texttt{tumor.grow()} according to a dosing schedule. At the genotype level the per-cell
stochastic effect becomes a dose-scaled modifier of a clone's effective rates, refreshed each
treated step so it never corrupts the shared genotype: death-rate therapies add a kill hazard to
sensitive clones (scaled by dose, drug effectiveness and $1-\text{treatment resistance}$), while
immunotherapy lowers a clone's immune resistance so the local immune microenvironment kills more.
Immune killing itself is modelled as additive contact pressure that rises with local immune-cell
density and is attenuated by immune resistance; the immune compartment is currently static, with
recruitment and migration dynamics left for future work. Treatments can be configured as adaptive
regimens that adjust dosing in response to tumor burden~\cite{west_survey_2023}.

\subsection*{The \texttt{sample} module}
This module corresponds to processing a tumor to prepare it for a data assay, via
\texttt{Biopsy} and \texttt{Dissociation} steps that select a subset of cells from the
simulated tumor while preserving their ground-truth state. A biopsy selects cells from one or
more spatial regions; dissociation applies cell-type-dependent recovery probabilities, so that
fragile types are under-represented and robust types enriched; and a liquid-biopsy mode biases
selection toward cells with a chosen phenotype (e.g.\ high dispersal). These steps reproduce the
multi-region under-sampling, composition bias and selective enrichment of real protocols
(\Cref{fig:sampling}).

\subsection*{The \texttt{data} module}
The data module generates data from the cells obtained through a biopsy or dissociation of the
simulated tumor. This includes bulk and single-cell DNA and RNA sequencing, as well as
spot-based spatial transcriptomics following the 10x Visium technology. Bulk and single-cell
DNA assays generate coverage and variant-allele counts; coverage is copy-number-dependent and
GC-biased, and the single-cell layer adds beta-binomial allele overdispersion and allelic
dropout. Single-cell RNA-seq counts are generated with a variable library size (lognormal) and
negative-binomial (gamma--Poisson) overdispersion, so that simulated counts exhibit the
cell-to-cell depth variation and overdispersion of real data. Spatial transcriptomics
aggregates the transcripts of cells falling within each spot of a regular grid placed over the
tissue slice, with spatially autocorrelated per-spot library sizes. Each modality can also be
emitted as aligned reads: a canonical per-locus allele map gives every genomic position a
reference and alternate base shared across modalities; a per-cell reference sequence is built by
duplicating, deleting and substituting bases according to copy number and variants; coverage is
allocated in proportion to copy number; and a single error-injection step partitions molecules
into reference and alternate before a third-party short-read simulator (DWGSIM or
ART~\cite{huang_art_2012} for DNA; a synthetic-transcriptome or scReadSim-style real-template
path~\cite{yan_screadsim_2023} for RNA) writes FASTQ, optionally aligned to BAM. Sharing the
allele map and the error step across modalities keeps a variant consistent --- same position,
same alternate base, single error source --- between DNA, RNA and spatial reads.

\subsection*{Inference}
\label{sec:inference_methods}
Two kinds of parameters can be inferred from data. Tumor-level evolutionary rates are estimated
by approximate Bayesian computation with random forests~\cite{raynal_abc_2019}: a reference table
of (parameters, summary statistics) pairs is built by simulating tumors, and a forest regresses
parameters on summaries to give point estimates and credible intervals for a target tumor; the
reference table may be built with the $\tau$-leaping update for speed. Assay-level technical
parameters are estimated by per-modality \texttt{estimate} routines using method-of-moments and
maximum likelihood on summary statistics --- coverage dispersion, GC bias, allelic dropout and
allele overdispersion for DNA; library-size variability and overdispersion for RNA; spatial-field
length-scale and per-spot concentration for Visium. The package ships defaults calibrated to such
fits; only scale-free, unit-reconciled parameters are transferred, while gene-set- and
unit-dependent quantities are re-fit per reference dataset.

\subsection*{Validation}
\label{sec:validation}
Validation scripts for each modality are distributed with the software in the \texttt{validation}
module and can be pointed at any reference dataset. Simulated single-cell RNA-seq is compared to a
real 10x dataset (PBMC3k) by count-realism statistics --- per-cell library size, the mean--variance
relationship, and the dropout curve (\Cref{fig:validation_scrna}). DNA is validated by fitting and
re-simulating coverage dispersion, GC bias, and single-cell allele spread with dropout against
synthetic and real references (GIAB HG002, Tapestri, DLP+; \Cref{fig:validation_dna}). Spatial data
is validated by the spatial autocorrelation (Moran's $I$, computed with
squidpy~\cite{palla_squidpy_2022}) of per-spot library size against a real 10x Visium section
(\Cref{fig:validation_visium}). Evolutionary dynamics, copy-number selection,
the neutral SNV spectrum, sampling biases, parameter recovery and $\tau$-leaping equivalence are
each validated by the correspondingly named scripts.
